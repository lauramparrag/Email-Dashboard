Email Do's & Don'ts
Distilled from your top and bottom 20% of campaigns

✓ What Works

Open with a bold POV or contrarian observation ("Speed Isn't a Strategy")
Use specific times in urgency emails: "tomorrow at 12 PM CT" beats "this week"
Write from a named expert's first-person perspective for webinar invites
Lead subject lines with a stat, direct question, or personal challenge
Keep body copy 150–250 words — the sweet spot for CTR and CTOR
Offer the recording in every follow-up — removes the "can't attend" objection
End with one single, clear CTA button

✗ What Hurts

"Webinar:" or "Live Webinar:" subject prefixes — your lowest-performing pattern
Very small list follow-ups under 200 sends — negligible ROI, skews metrics
Multiple competing CTAs in one email — dilutes click intent
Email 3 sent less than 48hrs before event — opt-out spikes visible in data
Body copy that recaps the previous email without a new angle or argument
Long product feature lists without a "so what" outcome statement

What Works With Our Audience
Patterns from our email data translated into content principles — useful for blog, LinkedIn, events, or any channel.
Content Angles That Drive Action
🔥 Highest engagement
AI governance — not AI capability
Our audience is already using AI translation. Content that helps them control, measure, and govern it drives far more action than content about what AI can do. They know what it can do. They need to know how to manage it.
✓ "How enterprises are managing AI translation risk"
✓ "COMET scoring and when human review still matters"
✗ "AI translation is transforming global content"
🔥 Highest engagement
The honest tradeoff
Content that names a real tension — without pretending it's solved — consistently outperforms content that only shows the upside. "Speed Isn't a Strategy for AI Translation" was our best-performing newsletter. "AI Translation Is Everywhere. Now What?" our best body copy opener.
✓ Name the tradeoff in the headline
✓ Acknowledge the problem before offering a path
✗ Lead with the solution before earning trust
Strong engagement
Specific compliance & regulatory risk
High-stakes, specific, and hard to find good guidance on. The China website compliance series had our highest open rates — because nobody else was covering it clearly. Regulatory + technical complexity = content they can't get anywhere else.
✓ Name the specific regulation or market
✓ Show the consequence of getting it wrong
✗ Generic "compliance considerations" framing
Strong engagement
"How we actually do this" > best practices
Operational reality consistently beats theory. "What we're seeing work in enterprise translation governance" outperforms "best practices for enterprise translation." They're practitioners. Write for people solving real problems, not people writing whitepapers.
✓ "What we're seeing across our clients right now"
✓ Name the specific problem, tool, or workflow
✗ "Best practices for AI-powered localization"
Moderate engagement
Stats work — when they're specific
"74% Say AI Is a Priority. Here's What to Do With That" pulled strong opens. But the stat needs to connect to an action or implication. A number alone isn't enough — it's the "so what" that makes them click.
✓ Stat + implication: "74% say X. Here's what that means"
✓ Specific number over vague majority: "74%" beats "most"
✗ Stat without a "so what" attached
Lower engagement
Product-first content
When we lead with what the product does, engagement drops. This audience responds to the business problem, not the technology. Product belongs in the proof — not the headline. It's the answer to "how," not the hook.
✗ "GlobalLink TMS helps you manage localization at scale"
✗ "Introducing our new AI quality estimation feature"
✓ Lead with the problem. Bring in product as evidence.
Tone & Voice
Write like a practitioner, not a vendor
The clearest signal in our data: first-person voice from a named expert drove our best-ever registration numbers. "Joe Campbell here. I run product for GlobalLink..." outperformed every corporate-voice email by a wide margin. Our audience is senior and skeptical. They respond to peers, not pitches.
Vendor "GlobalLink delivers enterprise-grade AI translation solutions"
Peer "Over the past year, I've watched AI translation move into every platform where content gets created — and most teams aren't ready for it"
Vendor "Join our experts to learn best practices for AI localization"
Peer "Got questions about AI in localization? Bring them here."
Be specific. Vague loses them instantly.
This audience reads fast and trusts specifics. A concrete detail signals that the content is worth reading — a vague claim signals it isn't. This applies to every channel: blog headlines, LinkedIn copy, event descriptions, ad creative.
Vague "Improve your translation quality"
Specific "How to catch AI translation errors before they reach production"
Vague "Scale your global content strategy"
Specific "What breaks when you push AI translation past 10 languages"
CTAs That Convert
One ask, not several
Every time we put multiple CTAs in front of this audience, click intent drops. Pick the one action that matters most and make it the only option. Everything else is noise.
Multiple CTAs = lower CTOR across all campaign types in our data
Action-specific language
They skip "Learn More" and "Read Now." Specific action language that tells them exactly what they'll get performs significantly better. The more the CTA sounds like a deliverable, the better.
✗ "Learn More" · "Read Now" · "Click Here"
✓ "Get the governance framework" · "Watch the 18-min session" · "Bring your questions"
Urgency needs a reason
Urgency with a specific anchor converts. Urgency without one feels like pressure. "Registration closes tonight at midnight" works. "Don't miss out" doesn't. A time, a date, a real closing window — that's what creates action.
"Tomorrow at 12 PM CT" emails: +7 point open rate lift vs. vague urgency
